\section{Dataset}

\field{Source:}{Optiver, ``Trading at the Close'', a Kaggle competition released in 2023. It records the last ten
minutes of the Nasdaq closing auction, the call auction that sets each stock's official closing price at 16:00, for
\nStocks{} stocks over \nDays{} trading days. One row is one stock at one 10-second snapshot between 15:50 and
15:59.}

\field{What is raw about it:}{the indicative clearing prices are missing for half the rows by exchange rule; four
stock-days carry no book at all; far prices reach hundreds of times the reference price; \nStocksWithGaps{} stocks
leave and re-enter the panel; the response is a 60-second move sampled every 10 seconds, so consecutive rows share
most of their window; and the index that the response is measured against is hidden.}

\minihead{Processing at stream scale}
A summary that is updated row by row, without storing the data, is how a live auction feed would be monitored. We
streamed all \nCleanRows{} rows through a Count-Min sketch of $\nCmDepth\times\nCmWidth$ counters
($\varepsilon=0.001$, $\delta=0.01$) to total the imbalance shares of each of the \nCmItems{} stock-days. The sketch
uses \nCmMemory{} of the memory of exact counting. Every estimate stayed within the $\varepsilon N$ guarantee, the
worst at \nCmMaxShare{} of it, and \nCmTop{} of the 10 heaviest stock-days were found.

\section{Basic questions and problem statement}

\minihead{Basic questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{How lopsided are auctions?} Sell imbalances appear in \nFlagSell{} of snapshots, buy in \nFlagBuy{},
  and \nFlagNone{} are balanced.
  \item \textbf{How costly is trading near the close?} The median quoted spread is \nSpreadMedian{} bps, and it
  narrows as 16:00 approaches (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}).
  \item \textbf{Is the target normal?} No (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}). Moves beyond 3 sd occur in \nTailObserved{} of
  rows, \nTailRatio{} times the normal \nTailNormal{} but inside Chebyshev's limit of \nTailCheb. A Student $t$ fitted
  by maximum likelihood has only \nTFitDf{} degrees of freedom: the variance exists, so the CLT and Chebyshev apply,
  but the fourth moment does not. It cuts the $\chi^2$ goodness-of-fit statistic from \nNormChi{} (normal) to
  \nTFitChi{}, yet is still rejected at this sample size.
  \item \textbf{Do imbalances change side at random?} No. A stock flips side \nFlipMean{} times a day on average, but
  the variance is \nFlipDispersion{} times the mean, and \nFlipZero{} of stock-days never flip against \nFlipZeroPoisson{}
  under a Poisson; $\chi^2=\nFlipChi$ on \nFlipDf{} d.f. Some auctions are one-sided all along, others churn.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_target_distribution}
  \caption{The target is far more peaked and heavy-tailed than a normal. Left: histogram on a log scale, with the
  normal of the same mean and spread in orange; the bars sit far above it in both tails. Right: Q-Q plot; normal data
  would lie on the line. So tests average to one value per day and rely on the CLT across days.}
  \label{fig:dist}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!t]

  \caption{The auction changes regime at 15:55. Each line is an average over all stocks and days at each 10\,s

Figure~\ref{fig:profile} points to 15:55. At that moment Nasdaq starts publishing the near and far indicative prices,
and it stops accepting new market-on-close orders. Balanced auctions rise from \nBalancedPre{} to \nBalancedPost{} of
stocks within ten seconds.

\minihead{Our problem statements}
\noindent\fbox{\begin{minipage}{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}
Overall question: is the closing-auction imbalance already in the price?

\smallskip
\textbf{PS1 (information).} Does the published imbalance carry information about the stock's next-minute move
against the index? \emph{(Tests 1 and 2)}

\textbf{PS2 (timing).} Does the 15:55 rule change make the imbalance more informative? \emph{(Test 3)}

\textbf{PS3 (risk).} Does the 15:55 rule change make the next minute less volatile? \emph{(Test 4)}

\textbf{PS4 (tradability).} Is that information worth money to an outsider who pays the half-spread?
\emph{(Tests 5 and 1)}

\smallskip
\textbf{How they link.} PS1 asks whether the signal exists. PS2 and PS3 ask whether 15:55 raises the signal and
lowers the noise. PS4 asks whether, together, this leaves money for an outsider; if it does not, the imbalance is
already in the price.
\end{minipage}}

\field{How they are novel:}{the basic questions read one column at a time. Ours ask whether public information is
already in the price, which needs an engineered signal, a day-level unit that respects shared index moves, and an
economic yardstick (the spread). PS2 and PS3 compare the same stocks on the same day before and after a rule change. The
public work on this dataset that we found fits predictive models; it does not test pricing, account for the overlap
or use the 15:55 boundary.}

\section{Statistical inferences}

\field{Rows tested:}{the \nTestRows{} snapshots at whole minutes (0, 60, \dots, 540\,s), so no two targets of a stock
overlap. Pre-15:55 rows are those at 0 to 240\,s, whose windows end by 15:55; post rows are those at 300 to 540\,s.
Tests 1 and 3 average to one value per trading day ($n=\nDays$), because stocks on the same day share market
conditions. Tests 2, 4 and 5 use the syllabus test for their question ($\chi^2$, F, one-proportion z) on rows. The
target is measured against the index, which removes the largest move all stocks share, but rows from one day are
still not fully independent, so these p-values may overstate the evidence. Each is paired with a day-level check in
its result, and every decision and verdict below holds at the day level.}

\field{Smallest difference that matters (SDM):}{the median quoted half-spread, \nSdm{} bps. Acting on the imbalance
means crossing at least half the spread to get in, and any real exit costs more. So the half-spread is the minimum
cost of trading the signal, which is generous to the edge: an edge below it cannot be traded. Each test states its own threshold; all were fixed before testing.}

\field{Reading a result:}{an effect \emph{matters} when its whole 95\% interval lies beyond the threshold, and
\emph{does not matter} when the whole interval lies inside it. We use $\alpha=0.05$ per test and report Holm-adjusted
p-values across the five. Because the row-level p-values of Tests 2, 4 and 5 may be too small, we also apply Holm to
each test's day-level p-value; it gives the same decision for \nHolmDayAgree{} of the 5 tests (Test 2, the closest,
at \nHolmDayTwo).}

\subsection*{Test 1 (PS1, PS4): does the imbalance side predict the move?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\mu\le0$ \\
$H_1$ & $\mu>0$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ \\
Side & one-sided (right) \\
Test & one-sample t-test on day means \\
Smallest difference that matters & \nSdm{} bps (the SDM) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

$\mu$ is the expected edge per trade, $E[\text{side}\times\text{target}]$, for a trader who buys on a buy imbalance
and sells on a sell imbalance. Each day gives one mean over its flagged snapshots. One-sided, because an efficient
market predicts zero and liquidity demand predicts a positive edge. The interval is two-sided because the verdict
needs its upper end.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
Day means are independent across days and, by the CLT, close to normal at $n=\nDays$ even though rows are
heavy-tailed; $\sigma$ is unknown, so a one-sample t-test fits.\par
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
z-test on all rows & treats \nOneNaiveN{} rows as independent, although rows from one day are not; it gives
$z=\nOneNaiveZ$, overstating the evidence. The \nOneOverlapN{} overlapping rows would be worse still \\
Welch t, buy vs sell rows & same question ($t=\nOneWelchT$, gap \nOneWelchDiff{} bps) with the same row dependence;
a Brown-Forsythe test (variance ratio \nOneVarRatio, p = \nOneBfP) finds the spreads differ, so Welch and not pooled;
the pooled t is \nOnePooledT{} anyway \\
Bootstrap over days & no normality needed; outside the syllabus, so a check: \nOneBootCi{} bps, same as the t interval \\
Permutation within snapshots & shuffles sides across stocks at each snapshot, \nPermOneN{} times; no shuffle came near
the observed edge (largest \nPermOneMax{} bps), p = \nPermOneP \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\tpart{c}{Result}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
Estimate & $+\nOneEst$ bps per trade (raw target: $+\nOneRawEst$) \\
95\% confidence interval & \nOneCi{} bps \\
Test statistic (d.f.) & $t=\nOneT$ (\nOneDf) \\
p-value (Holm) & \nOneP{} (\nHolmOne) \\
Decision & reject $H_0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The imbalance side predicts the next minute, on \nOnePositiveDays{} of \nDays{} days. The edge does not matter: even
the top of its interval is \nOneShareOfSdm{} of the half-spread, so the information is in the price to within trading
costs. For PS1 the imbalance does carry information; for PS4 the edge is too small to pay for the trade.

\subsection*{Test 2 (PS1): is the direction of the move independent of the side?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & side and direction are independent \\
$H_1$ & they are dependent \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ \\
Side & non-directional ($\chi^2$ uses its upper tail; the direction is read from the up-shares) \\
Test & $\chi^2$ test of independence, $3\times2$ table \\
Smallest difference that matters & Cramér's $V=\nTwoVThreshold$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

Test 1 asks about the size of the move; Test 2 asks only about its sign, which is robust to the heavy tails. We use
the first snapshot of each stock-day, at 15:50, fixed in advance: it is the first imbalance a trader sees, and each
stock appears once per day (\nTwoN{} rows; \nTwoZeroRows{} snapshots with no move, neither up nor down, are left
out). $V=0.10$ is the conventional floor for a small association. Buy and sell imbalances, and up and down moves, are
each close to half here, so $V$ for buy against sell equals the gap in up-share: $V=0.10$ is a gap of
\nTwoGapThreshold{} percentage points. PS1 is about buy against sell, so the verdict applies the threshold to that gap.
The full $3\times2$ table gives the same answer on its own terms: its $V$ (\nTwoV) is close to the buy-against-sell
value (\nTwoPhi), and its own interval (bootstrap below) also lies wholly below 0.10.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
Side (3 levels) and direction (2) are both categorical, the data are counts, and every expected count is far above 5
(smallest \nTwoMinExpected), so a $\chi^2$ test of independence fits.\par
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
Two-proportion z-test & drops the balanced rows; on buy vs sell it gives $z=\nTwoPropZ$ and an up-share gap of
\nTwoDiff{} points (95\% CI \nTwoDiffCi) \\
G-test & same large-sample limit; $G=\nTwoG$, same decision \\
$\chi^2$ on all \nTwoAllN{} minute rows & repeats each stock nine more times a day, so rows are not independent;
$\chi^2=\nTwoAllChi$ \\
Bootstrap over days & resamples whole days (outside the syllabus, as in Test 1); 95\% interval for $V$ \nTwoVCi, a
check on the interval below \\
$\chi^2$ at every minute & exploratory, run after the main test, so only described: $V$ is below 0.10 at
\nTwoVBelow{} of \nTwoMinutes{} minutes (largest \nTwoVOtherMax) but \nTwoVLast{} at 15:59, whose window contains the
16:00 closing cross \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\tpart{c}{Result}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
Estimate & $V=\nTwoV$; up-share \nTwoUpBuy{} after buy, \nTwoUpNone{} after balanced, \nTwoUpSell{} after sell
(gap \nTwoGap{} points) \\
95\% confidence interval & gap \nTwoGapCi{} points (t over days) \\
Test statistic (d.f.) & $\chi^2=\nTwoChi$ (\nTwoDf) \\
p-value (Holm) & \nTwoP{} (\nHolmTwo) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

At 15:50 the side and the direction are dependent, in the order PS1 expects (buy, then balanced, then sell), but
weakly: the whole interval for the gap lies below \nTwoGapThreshold{} points, so the association does not matter. Only
in the last minute does it reach a size that matters (gap \nTwoGapLast{} points, 95\% CI \nTwoGapLastCi), an
exploratory finding that agrees with the placebo test below. For PS1, the side carries the direction of the move,
but only weakly at 15:50.

\subsection*{Test 3 (PS2): does 15:55 change how predictable the move is?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\mu_\Delta=0$ \\
$H_1$ & $\mu_\Delta\neq0$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ \\
Side & two-sided \\
Test & paired t-test over days \\
Smallest difference that matters & $|\Delta\text{IC}|=\nThreeThreshold$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The information coefficient (IC) is the rank correlation, across stocks, between signed imbalance and the next move:
0 means no predictive power, 1 a perfect ranking. Each day gives an IC before and after 15:55, each pooling that
phase's five minute snapshots, and $\Delta_d$ is post minus pre. Two-sided, because the near price could absorb the
imbalance information or sharpen it. A change of 0.02 is about two-thirds of the IC before 15:55 (\nThreePre); a
smaller change leaves the signal about as useful as it was.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
The two ICs of a day come from the same stocks in the same market, so they are paired; the paired t-test removes the
day effect. The \nDays{} daily differences are close to normal (skew $\nThreeSkew$, excess kurtosis $\nThreeKurt$), so
the t-test's assumption holds.\par
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
Fisher z on two pooled correlations & assumes two independent samples, but they share stocks and days; gives
$z=\nThreeFisherZ$ \\
t-test for each correlation & tests each phase against zero, not the change; pre $r=\nThreePooledPre$
($t=\nThreePooledPreT$), post $r=\nThreePooledPost$ ($t=\nThreePooledPostT$) \\
Sign test over days & distribution-free; \nThreeSignDays{} of \nDays{} days rise, p = \nThreeSignP \\
Sign-flip permutation & randomly swaps each day's pre and post, \nFlipThreeN{} times; largest \nFlipThreeMax, p =
\nFlipThreeP \\

\subsection*{Stress tests, with criteria fixed before they ran}

\minihead{Is 15:55 really the cause? Placebo boundaries}
If the rule change drives Test 3, the IC should jump at 15:55 and nowhere else. We computed the jump at each of the
nine minute boundaries, as a paired t over days. The jump at 15:55 is real, $+\nJumpReal$ (95\% CI \nJumpRealCi), but
it is not the largest: the jump into the last minute is $+\nJumpLast$ (\nJumpLastCi). Our pre-set criterion fails.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two rules change at once, next to the close.} At 15:55 the near price appears and market-on-close
  entry closes; the placebo test shows the post-15:55 gain is mostly the final minute, and without it the change is
  inconclusive, so time to the close and the rule change are not separated.
  \item \textbf{Association, not cause.} The imbalance may predict because the same traders move both, not because
  traders read it.
  \item \textbf{Rows dropped and rows unused.} \nEmptyRows{} rows with no book were dropped and \nFarOutliers{}
  impossible far prices set to missing. To avoid overlapping targets the tests use one snapshot a minute, 10 of every
  55, and Test 2 only the first; results for the other snapshots are inferred, not tested.
  \item \textbf{Rows are not independent.} Tests 2, 4 and 5 run the syllabus test on rows from the same days, so
  their p-values may be too small; we rely on their day-level checks, which agree. Days themselves are treated as
  independent, although market-wide news can link consecutive days; the effects are large enough that modest
  dependence would not change a decision.
  \item \textbf{Some findings are exploratory.} The last-minute lead, the per-minute pattern in Test 2 and the
  announcement windows in Test 4 were found after looking at the data, so they need confirming on new data.
  \item \textbf{Costs are approximate.} The half-spread is a snapshot quote; fees, impact, index hedging and auction
  fills are not modelled, so the true cost is higher. This strengthens Test 5 and weakens the last-minute lead.
  \item \textbf{One exchange, anonymised period.} Dates and tickers are hidden, so results cover these 200 Nasdaq
  stocks over about two years.
\end{itemize}
